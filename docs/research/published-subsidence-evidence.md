# Published InSAR and Subsidence Evidence in Uttar Pradesh

**Status:** Peer-reviewed studies located and their abstracts checked on 2026-11-26. Full texts were not independently audited or reproduced. This is a literature summary, not an AquaInSAR result.

## Claim boundary

The studies below report surface deformation for their own study areas, periods, processing choices, and reference networks. They are evidence that InSAR has detected subsidence in parts of Uttar Pradesh. They are **not** evidence that:

- the same signal exists in an agricultural candidate area;
- groundwater extraction is the sole or dominant cause everywhere;
- a reported rate transfers to another district, block, or aquifer;
- AquaInSAR's method, pipeline, or product is validated;
- NISAR can provide a multi-year validated trend today.

InSAR estimates changes in satellite-to-ground range. A line-of-sight (LOS) rate is not automatically vertical subsidence, because horizontal motion, viewing geometry, and processing choices contribute. A correlation with groundwater level is an association, not proof of causation.

## What the evidence supports

1. **Local subsidence has been published in Uttar Pradesh.** The strongest verified example in this pass is urban Lucknow, with a 2025 multi-sensor study reporting more than about 36 mm/year in specific neighborhoods.
2. **Groundwater decline and deformation co-occur in the study areas.** Lucknow studies report spatial correspondence and correlation with groundwater levels; Varanasi reports a coupling with piezometric and hydroclimatic data.
3. **Aquifer compaction is a plausible mechanism in alluvial settings.** The Lucknow studies discuss compressible aquifer materials and skeletal storativity. This is consistent with the general mechanism, but it remains study-specific.
4. **A method precedent exists.** Sentinel-1 PSInSAR/InSAR with CGWB groundwater levels and GNSS/in-situ context has been attempted in UP. That supports feasibility of the general approach, not the feasibility of a specific agricultural pilot.

## What the evidence does not support

- **A state-wide subsidence map.** The studies cover specific urban/riverfront areas, not all of UP.
- **An agricultural-pixel validation.** In the studies verified for this dossier, the reported results are urban or riverfront. This pass did not verify a peer-reviewed agricultural-pixel subsidence validation in UP.
- **A transferable rate.** Rates depend on aquifer materials, pumping history, period, sensor, processing, and reference network.
- **Causation for a future AquaInSAR pilot.** The studies' causal language is the authors' interpretation for their sites. AquaInSAR must test alternatives and report uncertainty.
- **Validation of AquaInSAR.** No AquaInSAR processing or ground comparison exists in the repository.
- **A multi-year NISAR trend.** NISAR's validated L-band record starts on 2026-10-21, so it is too short for a multi-year trend as of this snapshot.

## Why Lucknow is not automatically the pilot

Lucknow has the strongest published signal and the best-known method precedent. It is also urban/peri-urban, while the project intends to study an agricultural region. A Lucknow study could serve as:

- a **method benchmark** for processing, reference-network selection, and uncertainty reporting;
- a **communication example** of why groundwater-related subsidence matters;
- a **comparison case** for an agricultural candidate.

It would not satisfy the agricultural research question by itself. Varanasi has published riverfront evidence and long groundwater context, but the reported motion is modest and the setting is riverfront/urban. Western UP candidates may have strong groundwater-stress indicators but did not yield a district-specific, independently validated agricultural subsidence signal in this pass. No candidate is selected or ranked here.

## Cross-study caveats

- **Different periods and sensors.** The studies span 2014–2024 with different Sentinel-1 archives, track geometries, and processing chains.
- **Different vertical assumptions.** Some studies use ascending and descending data; others may report LOS rates or assume vertical motion. The abstracts do not always state the decomposition.
- **Different reference networks.** A stable reference choice changes the absolute rate. Check the reference area and datum.
- **Different groundwater data.** CGWB measurement dates, well types, screen depths, and interpolation differ across studies.
- **Atmospheric and orbit errors.** Tropospheric delay, ionospheric effects (especially L-band), orbit errors, DEM errors, and unwrapping errors can produce or mask trends.
- **Seasonal and anthropogenic signals.** Soil moisture, irrigation, construction, and seasonal loading can contribute. A multi-year trend can mix seasonal and long-term components.
- **Publication and selection bias.** A published subsidence study is more likely where a signal was found. Quiet or negative areas are less represented.
- **Abstract-only review.** This dossier did not read the full methods, supplementary material, or reviewer responses for the paywalled papers. Numbers are as reported in the abstract.

## What to check before citing a study in a deliverable

- [ ] Full citation, DOI, and publication year.
- [ ] Study boundary and period.
- [ ] Sensor, track/pass, polarization, and acquisition count.
- [ ] Processing method (PSInSAR, SBAS, other) and software.
- [ ] Whether the rate is LOS or vertical; if vertical, the decomposition method.
- [ ] Reference network and datum.
- [ ] Coherence threshold and masking.
- [ ] Atmospheric correction and orbit handling.
- [ ] Ground-reference data, frequency, and spatial matching.
- [ ] Uncertainty, statistical significance, and validation design.
- [ ] Whether the reported number is an observation, a correlation, or a forecast.
- [ ] Whether the study area is urban, riverfront, or agricultural.
- [ ] What the authors explicitly say about causation and alternatives.
